Wildlife rehabilitation

A rescue only matters if there is somewhere safe for recovery to happen.

Wildlife rehabilitation follows a chain from intake and assessment through diagnosis, treatment, suitable housing, recovery, translocation and release. The project focuses on strengthening the facilities and systems behind every stage.

From arrival to release

Care is a sequence, not a single intervention.

The project identifies rescue of injured wildlife, hospitalisation, rehabilitation, release, management of disabled animals, capture and translocation as core activities. Together they form a clear operational path from arrival to release.

01Intake

Receive the animal safely and record the circumstances of rescue.

02Triage

Stabilise urgent cases and prioritise immediate care.

03Diagnostics

Use appropriate clinical assessment and diagnostic capacity.

04Treatment

Provide surgery, medication, nutrition and supportive care as required.

05Recovery

Move animals into suitable spaces that support natural behaviour.

06Release

Prepare, transport and return releasable wildlife to suitable habitat.

What the project needs

Facilities determine how much good care is actually possible.

The project identifies constraints around capacity, treatment equipment, suitable shelter, diagnostics and release planning. Improving these areas can strengthen care from intake through recovery.

Outpatient and triage capacity

A quiet, controlled intake area reduces stress and gives staff a place to assess new arrivals before they enter the main rehabilitation environment.

Surgery and diagnostics

Clinical decisions are stronger when veterinary teams have suitable operating space, equipment, laboratory support and diagnostic tools.

Suitable housing

Birds, reptiles, primates, deer, small cats and other patients need different enclosure sizes, substrates, climbing or perching structures, temperature conditions and levels of isolation.

Nursery and recovery

Orphaned or very young animals require controlled temperature, nutrition and monitoring, while recovering adults need space to heal without unnecessary disturbance.

Translocation and release

Safe transport equipment, containment, release site assessment and planning after release are essential parts of rehabilitation.

Staff and operating systems

Facilities work only when trained people can coordinate records, rescue calls, treatment protocols, overnight care and collaboration with other centres.

A centre within a landscape

Rehabilitation should end with the right destination.

Release requires medical fitness, natural behaviour and suitable habitat. Some animals may instead need longer term care when a safe return to the wild is not possible.

The project also identifies collaboration with other rehabilitation centres, awareness to reduce harmful human interference, and protocols for treatment, management and release as part of improving outcomes.

Wildlife partners

Equipment, veterinary expertise and release capacity can all change outcomes.

Partnerships can focus on a defined rehabilitation bottleneck rather than trying to solve the entire system at once.
